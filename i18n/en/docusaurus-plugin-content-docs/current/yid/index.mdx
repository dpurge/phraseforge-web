# Yiddish language

## Genetic and structural classification

Indo-European family, Germanic branch, West Germanic subgroup. Descended from Middle High German, with significant Hebrew-Aramaic, Romance, and Slavic layers.

## Number of users and geographic distribution

About 600,000 - 1 million speakers. Hasidic and Orthodox communities in the USA (New York), Israel, the United Kingdom, Belgium, and Argentina. Before the Holocaust — around 11 million speakers in Central and Eastern Europe.

## Dialects

Two large groups: Western Yiddish (Germany, the Netherlands, Alsace — largely extinct) and Eastern Yiddish, divided into: Northeastern (Lithuanian — *Litvish*), Central (Polish — *Poylish*), and Southeastern (Ukrainian — *Galitsianish*). The literary standard (*klal-shprakh*) is based mainly on Lithuanian phonetics and the orthography agreed in 1937 by YIVO.

## Phonetics

Rich vowel system (dialectal differences are significant — e.g. *yidish* / *yiddish*). Dynamic, usually non-final stress.

## Writing system

Hebrew alphabet (22 letters) with modifications: full vowel letters (*alef, ayin, vov, yud*) — Yiddish spelling, unlike Hebrew, is phonemic. The YIVO standard was set in 1937.

## History

Separated from German in the 10th-11th centuries in the Rhineland, with the spread of Jewish settlement. Early Yiddish (to the 15th c.), Classical (15th-18th c. — *Mayse-bukh*, *Tsene-rene*), and Modern (19th-20th c. — Mendele, Sholem Aleichem, I.B. Singer). The Holocaust broke the linguistic continuity in Europe; postwar centers — the USA and Israel (Hasidism).
